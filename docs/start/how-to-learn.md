# How to learn this without losing again

You didn't struggle with university math because you can't do math. You were good at high-school math, and the skills are the same. What changed is the *format*. University math moves at the pace of a lecture, assumes you'll fill gaps on your own, and is written in proofs nobody taught you to read. That's a fixable problem.

## 1. Use the three-pass method

Never try to absorb a lesson in one go.

| Pass | What you do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Skim** | Read only the intuition boxes, look at the figures, play with the interactive figures. Ignore the proofs. | 10–15 min |
| **2. Work** | Read everything except the collapsed *deeper* proofs. Redo every worked example on paper **before** reading its solution. | 45–90 min |
| **3. Own** | Close the page. Write down the main definitions and theorems from memory. Do the exercises. Then read the deeper proofs. | 30–60 min |

Pass 3 can happen on a different day, and it's better if it does.

## 2. Read proofs actively

A proof is not something you read; it's something you *re-do*. For every proof:

1. **What's given? What's the goal?** Write both down.
2. **What's the strategy?** Direct? Contradiction? Induction? Usually the first line tells you.
3. **Check every step.** At each "therefore", ask *why*? If you can't answer, that step is where your understanding has a hole. Mark it.
4. **Summarize the proof in one sentence.** For example: "Cauchy–Schwarz: a quadratic in $t$ that is never negative has a non-positive discriminant." If you can do this, you'll remember the proof forever.

!!! intuition "Proofs are compressed explanations"
    A proof is the reason something is true, written as tightly as possible. Your job is to decompress it. That's why it feels slow, and that's normal. A professional mathematician reads one page of a proof in 20 minutes, not 2.

## 3. Keep a confusion log

Keep a notebook (or a text file) with one line per confusion:

> *2026-11-24, Derivatives: why does the chain rule proof need $g(x+h) \neq g(x)$?*

Half of these resolve themselves a lesson or two later. The rest are exactly the questions to ask an AI assistant or look up. Re-reading the log a month later is a great confidence boost: everything in it will look obvious.

## 4. Use AI as a tutor, not as a crutch

AI is extremely useful here, *if* you use it in the right order:

- ✅ **After** you've tried: "Here is my proof that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational. Is it correct? Where is it weak?"
- ✅ For alternate angles: "Explain eigenvectors using a physical example," or "Give me 5 more exercises like this, with solutions hidden."
- ✅ For checking computations: "Compute this Jacobian so I can compare with mine."
- ❌ Before you've tried: you'll feel like you understand, and then fail the exercise. Understanding you didn't produce yourself doesn't stick.

## 5. When you're stuck

- **The 20-minute rule.** Struggle honestly for 20 minutes. That struggle is what builds the understanding. After 20 minutes, look at the hint or the solution, *then close it and redo the problem from scratch*.
- **Step back one level.** Stuck on eigenvalues? The problem is almost always one prerequisite back (here, determinants or null spaces). Each lesson's header lists what it needs.
- **Change representation.** Draw a picture. Try $n = 1, 2, 3$. Plug in numbers. Most "aha" moments come from a concrete example.
- **Skip and come back.** Proofs marked *deeper* can wait. Moving on and returning in a week is a legitimate strategy, not a failure.

## 6. Pace yourself

Sustainable beats intense. Some suggested rhythms:

| Rhythm | Pace | Whole site |
|---|---|---|
| Steady | 5 hours/week (e.g. 45 min on weekdays) | ~8 months |
| Focused | 10 hours/week | ~4 months |
| ML fast track (see [roadmap](roadmap.md)) | 5 hours/week | ~4 months |

**End every session on a win.** Stop after finishing an exercise you solved, not in the middle of one you're stuck on. You'll come back more willingly.

## 7. Review with spacing

Two days after finishing a lesson, redo two of its exercises from memory. Do it again two weeks later. This takes 15 minutes and is the single most effective way to make knowledge permanent. The "Mark lesson complete" button is a good moment to plan your first review.

## 8. Remember what "understanding" means

You understand a concept when you can:

- [ ] state its definition precisely,
- [ ] give an example *and* a non-example,
- [ ] draw a picture of it,
- [ ] explain why the main theorem about it is true (sketch of proof),
- [ ] use it to solve a problem you haven't seen before.

This is a high bar. You don't need to clear it on the first pass. Clear it eventually, and you will never lose this knowledge again.
